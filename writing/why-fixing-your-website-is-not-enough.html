Why “just fix the website” does not fix AI visibility

A well-built website is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. The gap is where most of the work sits.

By Aaron Stalberg  ·  22 September 2026

The most common advice about AI visibility is to fix your own website. Get the structured data right, state what you do, publish good content. All of that is correct, and none of it is enough on its own.

I measured 37 sites for how often assistants named them in their own category. The results did not line up with anything I expected.

What did not predict it

  • Domain authority. Some of the highest-authority sites in the set were named rarely. Two of the lowest were named consistently.
  • Traffic. Organic visibility and AI visibility came apart repeatedly. Sites with a fraction of the traffic were quoted more.
  • Site quality. Fast, well-designed, technically excellent, and sometimes invisible. One of the best-built sites in the set was absent from every answer I checked.
  • Content volume. Hundreds of blog posts did not beat one page that answered a narrow question completely.

What did

The distinction that held up was not how good the site was. It was how Describable the company was, and how consistently that description appeared in places the site did not control.

  • A single unambiguous entity. The models could resolve the company to one thing. Where a name was shared, or a brand was ambiguous, the company got dropped rather than guessed at.
  • Independent corroboration. Third-party sources that said the same thing as the site. This mattered more than the site itself.
  • Being quotable. A specific, dated, attributable claim. Vague positioning gave a model nothing to lift.
  • Owning one narrow question. The sites that were named most were often not the biggest. They were the definitive answer to something specific.
The pattern is that AI visibility is largely a property of your reputation Elsewhere, what other sources say about you and whether they agree. Your site is where you make that easy to verify, not where you create it.

Why the usual advice stops short

Website advice is popular because it is actionable and entirely within your control. That is also its limitation. Most of what determines whether an assistant names you happens off your domain, in sources you do not own and often cannot edit.

Which means the work splits into three kinds, and only the first is familiar:

  1. On your site. State plainly what you do, to whom, and where. Make the entity unambiguous. Publish things worth citing. Necessary, and the easy part.
  2. Off your site, fixable. Directories and profiles that are wrong, inconsistent or missing. Tedious, high-yield, and almost nobody does it.
  3. Off your site, not yours. Trade press, archives, review sites. Requires asking someone else, and often the only route to correcting a wrong answer.

The caveat

37 sites is a small sample, chosen rather than randomly sampled, and measured at one moment. I would not dress it up as research. But the direction was consistent enough that I stopped expecting site quality to predict anything, and started looking at consistency and corroboration instead.

If you only fix your website, you will have done the part that was never the bottleneck.
